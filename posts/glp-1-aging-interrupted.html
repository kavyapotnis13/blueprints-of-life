GLP-1: Aging Interrupted

GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide have already reshaped how doctors treat diabetes, obesity, and cardiovascular risk. Now, a new study suggests these drugs might be doing something far less expected: slowing the molecular processes that make the body age. While the finding is preliminary, it points to a much bigger question in medicine, whether a single class of drugs originally designed to manage blood sugar could eventually be repurposed to extend healthy years of life.

The study, led by Michael Corley of UC San Diego and published in Nature Communications, is the first randomized, placebo-controlled trial to connect semaglutide with measurable changes in epigenetic aging markers. Rather than studying the general population, the researchers focused on 108 adults with HIV who also had lipohypertrophy, a condition marked by excess abdominal fat. HIV was a strategic choice: the virus is already known to accelerate biological aging, making its effects easier to detect and measure over a relatively short trial window. Half the participants received semaglutide injections over 32 weeks, while the other half received a placebo.

To measure aging, the researchers turned to DNA methylation, a pattern of chemical tags attached to DNA that influence which genes are turned on or off without changing the underlying genetic sequence. As people age, these methylation patterns shift in predictable ways, and scientists have built “epigenetic clocks” that use those shifts to estimate a person's biological age, which can differ from their actual chronological age. The team applied several of these clocks, each tuned to reflect aging in different systems, including the brain, heart, liver, and kidneys.

The results showed a consistent pattern across multiple clocks, participants on semaglutide aged more slowly than those on the placebo. The most striking figure came from the DunedinPACE clock, a measure of the pace of aging, which showed roughly a nine percent slowdown in the treatment group. Importantly, the study's authors were careful not to overstate the finding. Corley emphasized that the drug is not reversing aging or making people younger, but rather producing a signal that some of the biological processes tied to aging may be slowing down.

What makes this result interesting is not just the outcome, but the plausible mechanisms behind it. Semaglutide is already known to reduce chronic inflammation, improve metabolic function, and shrink visceral fat, the fat that surrounds internal organs and is strongly linked to age-related disease. All three of these factors are known drivers of biological aging, particularly in people with HIV, where persistent inflammation is common even when the virus itself is well controlled. Corley also pointed to newer evidence suggesting GLP-1 drugs may reprogram certain cells across different organs, which could help explain why the aging signal appeared across so many separate epigenetic clocks rather than just one.

This trial does not stand alone. A smaller pilot study published earlier this year in npj Aging found similar results in people with HIV and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, including slower biological aging and longer telomeres, the protective caps on chromosomes that shorten with each cell division. Two independent studies pointing in the same direction strengthens the case that the effect is real rather than a statistical fluke, though it is still far from proof that semaglutide meaningfully changes long-term health outcomes.

The choice to study people with HIV also carries significance beyond that population. Because accelerated aging processes tend to show up earlier and more intensely in people with HIV, this group can act as an early warning system for interventions that might later prove useful for the general public. If a treatment can slow biological aging in a population where that aging is already sped up, it becomes a reasonable next step to ask whether the same treatment could benefit people without HIV as well.
