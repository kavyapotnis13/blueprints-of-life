Drew Endy and the Case for Engineering Biology

Most scientists study how living things work. Drew Endy wants to make living things easier to build. A Stanford bioengineer and a pioneer of synthetic biology, he has argued for two decades that biology should become a true engineering discipline, with standard parts, shared tools, and open access. His work spans labs, classrooms, and policy rooms, which makes him as much an institution builder as a researcher.

Endy began as an engineer. A senior-year college class convinced him that living matter could serve as a building material. He earned his PhD at Dartmouth in 1997 with work on the bacteriophage T7, building a computer simulation of how this bacterial virus grows. That was an early sign of his instinct to treat biology as a system that can be modeled, predicted, and eventually designed. A virus with a compact genome is a manageable system, and understanding it well enough to simulate it is a first step toward engineering it.

That instinct deepened at MIT, where he worked with Thomas Knight, Gerald Jay Sussman, Randy Rettberg, and others on standardized biological components, devices, and parts known as BioBricks. Endy aims to bring to biology the kind of standardization other engineering fields enjoy. The analogy to software is central. BioBricks work like a genetic shorthand, much as programmers use languages such as Java instead of writing out raw bits, sparing engineers from assembling DNA one letter at a time. His paper "Foundations for engineering biology" helped lay out the case.

Standard parts are only useful if people share them, so Endy embraced openness over patent walls. He promoted a free registry of building blocks, and he has served since 2006 as president of the BioBricks Foundation. He also co-founded the iGEM competition, the International Genetically Engineered Machine contest. Teams draw BioBricks from the foundation's Registry of Standard Parts, contribute new ones back, and use them to engineer organisms that perform particular functions. The contest has grown into a global event that involves thousands of students every year.

Education runs through everything Endy does. He helped launch new undergraduate bioengineering majors at both MIT and Stanford, and he has served as a director of the iGEM Foundation since 2019. He has also tried to excite high school and college students through unconventional means, including comics about synthetic biology. He has discussed teaching biological literacy through an idea he calls the Labrary. The goal is a generation that sees biology not as a fixed set of facts but as a technology people can learn to work with.

At Stanford, where he became a Terman Fellow in 2008, Endy's lab pursues first-principles modeling paired with bottom-up experiments, aiming for design-build-work frameworks that go beyond tinker-and-test approaches. He has called biology an ideal technology for connecting energy, information, and physical matter. His influence extends into policy. He chaired the U.S. delegation to the Six Parties Symposia on Synthetic Biology in 2011 and 2012, served on an IUCN taskforce on synthetic biology and biodiversity conservation from 2018 to 2021, and has sat on the faculty council of the Stanford Emerging Technology Review since 2022.

Endy is also a candid critic of his own field. He has observed that biotechnology has so far delivered only a few of the grand promises made in the 1970s, after recombinant DNA was first achieved. His answer is not more hype but better infrastructure: standardized parts, open collaboration, trained engineers, and models predictive enough to replace trial and error. The challenge, as he sees it, is cultural and institutional as much as technical. Whether or not biology ever becomes as routine to engineer as software or bridges, Endy has already changed how a generation thinks about the possibility. What began as a thesis on a virus has grown into a movement built on a simple idea: if biology is going to be a technology, it should be a shared one.
