Drew Weissman and the Molecule That Learned to Hide

When COVID-19 swept the world, two vaccines using base-modified mRNA were developed at record speed. Behind that speed lay years of quiet basic research at the University of Pennsylvania, much of it carried out by immunologist Drew Weissman and his colleague Katalin Karikó. Their work solved a problem that had stalled mRNA medicine for years.

Weissman was born in 1959 in Lexington, Massachusetts, and trained as a physician-scientist, holding both an MD and a PhD. He is the Roberts Family Professor of Vaccine Research at Penn's Perelman School of Medicine, and he is affiliated with the Penn Institute for RNA Innovations. His laboratory studies RNA and innate immune biology and applies the findings to vaccine research and gene therapy.

The partnership that changed medicine began as a working relationship. Karikó had a new colleague at her university in the immunologist Weissman. She began producing mRNA for his research, and the two soon started collaborating on mRNA vaccine studies. The pairing was natural: she understood RNA, and he understood the immune system that RNA would have to get past.

The obstacle was immune reaction. Their early studies showed that mRNA is highly immunogenic, triggering immune responses that undermined its usefulness. A vaccine that sets off the body's alarms before it can deliver its message is of little use. Karikó noticed that transfer RNA did not provoke the same reaction. Nucleoside bases in mammalian RNA are often chemically modified, whereas mRNA made in a test tube is not. Perhaps that difference mattered.

It did. In 2005 they reported that inserting modified nucleosides, such as pseudouridine, into mRNA let the molecule evade immediate immune detection and stay active longer. Follow-up studies in 2008 and 2010 showed that delivering base-modified mRNA markedly increased protein production compared with unmodified mRNA. The modification solved two problems at once: less inflammation and more of the desired protein.

Here is why that mattered. An mRNA vaccine is essentially a set of instructions that cells read to make a harmless piece of a pathogen, which the immune system then learns to recognize. Once scientists know a pathogen's genetic sequence, those instructions can be written quickly, which is part of why mRNA vaccines could be developed so fast in 2020. But the approach only works if the body's alarm system doesn't destroy the instructions first, which is exactly the problem the modification solved.

Weissman and Karikó saw the promise early. They patented the technology in 2005 and started a company called RNARx. Weissman went on to explore other applications. He and colleagues were among the first to show, in mice and monkeys, that a modified mRNA vaccine delivered in lipid nanoparticles could induce immune defense against Zika virus. Those nanoparticles, tiny fatty bubbles that carry mRNA into cells, would later become part of the COVID-19 vaccines.

Recognition arrived in 2023. Karikó and Weissman jointly won the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for their discoveries about nucleoside base modifications that enabled effective mRNA vaccines against COVID-19. They split a prize worth roughly $989,000, and the award came just two years after the vaccines were approved, remarkably quick by the Nobel committee's standards. The best-known vaccines using the technology were made by BioNTech/Pfizer and Moderna.

Their findings changed how scientists understand the way mRNA interacts with the immune system and laid the groundwork for future uses of mRNA technology. Weissman's story is a reminder that world-changing medicine often starts with a basic question, in this case why one kind of RNA set off alarms and another didn't, and the patience to keep asking it.
