Frances Arnold and the Art of Directed Evolution

For much of the twentieth century, chemists who wanted better enzymes tried to design them. Frances Arnold took a different route and let evolution do the work. Directed evolution, which she pioneered in the early 1990s, creates new and better enzymes in the laboratory using the principles of evolution, and it earned her the 2018 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Today it is used worldwide in both industry and academia.

Arnold was born in Pittsburgh in 1956. She earned a bachelor's degree in mechanical and aerospace engineering at Princeton in 1979 and a doctorate in chemical engineering at Berkeley in 1985, then arrived at Caltech. She has taught there since 1986. When she won the Nobel, she was the Linus Pauling Professor of Chemical Engineering, Bioengineering and Biochemistry. Her engineering background shows in her approach. Rather than waiting to understand every detail of a protein, she treated enzyme improvement as an optimization problem that nature already knew how to solve.

The difficulty was that in the late 1980s and early 1990s, using enzymes to drive chemical reactions was hard, because the usual approach required working out from first principles how to change an enzyme. Rebuilding enzymes proved difficult given the enormous variation in protein structure, so Arnold turned to nature's method instead. Her process repeatedly introduces random mutations and rapidly screens the results so that beneficial changes accumulate. In 1993, she carried out the first directed evolution of enzymes.

One early experiment illustrates the idea. She introduced many random mutations into the genes of bacteria that produced subtilisin E, then exposed the mutated enzymes to an environment containing both DMF and casein. DMF is an organic solvent, so the aim was an enzyme that worked in conditions it never faced in nature. A Berkeley colleague explained the payoff this way: rather than designing a process around an existing enzyme's requirements, directed evolution fits the catalyst to the best manufacturing conditions.

Arnold and her coworkers then extended the technique to reactions that no enzyme had catalyzed before. They evolved enzymes that make bonds which do not occur in biology, such as carbon-silicon and carbon-boron bonds. Her lab effectively coaxed living cells into building chemical bonds absent from the natural world. It is a striking reversal: the process that shaped life is now used to teach life new chemistry. Arnold has said that evolution is the most powerful engineering method in the world and that we should use it to find biological solutions to problems.

The practical consequences are large. Enzymes produced through directed evolution are used in biofuels, agricultural chemicals, paper products, and pharmaceuticals, and they have replaced toxic chemicals in many industrial processes. Caltech reported that hundreds of laboratories and companies use the approach to make products ranging from laundry detergent to medicine. Arnold also cofounded two companies based on her work, a reminder that her research moved quickly from the bench to the marketplace.

Recognition followed. In 2011 she received both the Charles Stark Draper Prize and the National Medal of Technology and Innovation, and she was the first woman to win the Draper Prize from the National Academy of Engineering. Her Nobel made her the fifth woman to win the chemistry prize. She received half, sharing the award with George P. Smith and Gregory Winter. She became director of Caltech's Rosen Bioengineering Center in 2013.

She was woken by a 4 a.m. phone call with the news. At a Caltech press conference, she credited the fearlessness of the research culture there and her young colleagues, saying the prize belongs to the whole team. She also predicted a steady stream of chemistry Nobels for women. Arnold's legacy is both a method and a mindset. When a problem is too complex to solve by design, let variation and selection explore the possibilities for you. In a field that long prized rational design, she showed that humility before complexity can be a powerful engineering strategy.
