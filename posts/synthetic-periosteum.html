Rebuilding the Periosteum

When a patient loses a large piece of bone to cancer or severe trauma, surgeons often fill the gap with an allograft, a section of bone donated by someone else. Allografts are considered the gold standard for massive defects, yet about 60 percent fail within ten years, largely because they never integrate properly with the patient's own bone. Bioengineer Danielle Benoit has spent much of her career trying to change that number.

The problem, as Benoit frames it, is what donor bone is missing. Autografts, taken from a patient's own body, heal completely, and the periosteum, a thin layer of tissue wrapped around bone, orchestrates that recovery. Allografts, by contrast, are processed so thoroughly that no living stem cells or periosteum remain. Without that layer, the graft is a strong but largely inert scaffold. If the periosteum is what makes healing work, the logic follows that it could be rebuilt synthetically and wrapped around the graft before implantation.

Benoit began developing this idea at the University of Rochester, where she joined the faculty in 2010. She now continues it as the Lorry Lokey Chair of Bioengineering at the University of Oregon. In 2013, the National Institutes of Health awarded her a five-year grant to build periosteum mimetics from synthetic poly(ethylene glycol), or PEG, hydrogels designed to carry mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) to the surface of an allograft. Her broader body of work has earned her an NSF CAREER Award and election as a Fellow of the National Academy of Inventors.

The design is clever. The PEG hydrogel is photopolymerized around a decellularized allograft in a custom mold, with stem cells mixed in before the gel sets. It acts as a temporary extracellular matrix, giving the transplanted cells a place to live on the bone's surface while the donor graft keeps its structural strength. Crucially, the gel's degradation can be tuned to match the pace of host healing, allowing the patient's own bone and blood vessels to grow in. The lab's designs also call for bone-binding peptides to keep the hydrogel firmly attached. Because the material is synthetic, researchers can change one variable at a time, such as degradation rate or the cell types inside, which makes it a tool for discovery as well as a therapy.

Tests in mice have been encouraging. In a segmental femur defect model, allografts wrapped in the engineered periosteum showed greater vascularization, more callus bone, bridging ossification, and higher biomechanical strength than untreated allografts over sixteen weeks. Benoit and doctoral student Michael Hoffman then pushed further. To imitate the growth factor output of an autograft, they differentiated half of the transplanted cells toward bone before implantation. That mixed population sped up cartilage resorption, bone formation, and graft-host integration compared with either unmodified stem cells or bone-committed cells alone.

Perhaps the most important lesson concerns how the periosteum actually works. The results underscore that paracrine factors, signaling molecules secreted by cells, orchestrate host cell recruitment and new bone formation. Benoit's recent talks emphasize design features that optimize how long transplanted cells persist and how well they produce these factors. That understanding points beyond cell transplantation. A stated goal of her NIH-funded work was to isolate the periosteum's critical healing factors and eventually develop cell-free therapies. Her group also explores targeted drug delivery to recruit and expand the bone's own periosteal cells.

The stakes are considerable. Bone grafting procedures cost over $2.5 billion a year in the United States, and about a third rely on decellularized allografts. Even a modest improvement in how those grafts heal could spare patients repeat surgeries and failed implants. Much of the evidence so far comes from mouse studies, so the synthetic periosteum remains preclinical, and questions about scaling, cost, and safety in people are still ahead. Still, Benoit's work shows a compelling principle: rather than replacing bone with something inert, engineers can recreate the supportive environment that living tissue provides and let the body do the rest of the healing.
